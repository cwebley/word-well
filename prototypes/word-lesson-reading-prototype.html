Today's word

candid

/ˈkændɪd/ · adjective

In brief

Honest and direct, even when the truth may be uncomfortable.

Candid is usually approving: it names openness that is useful, clear, and proportionate, not bluntness for its own sake.

In a sentence

At work

“Her candid feedback helped the team improve the proposal.”

With a friend

“He was candid about why the plan had not worked.”

In an interview

“The memoir is candid about the cost of ambition.”

Where it comes from

Candid comes from Latin candidus, “white, bright, shining.” Roman candidates wore white togas as a public sign of openness, which helped the word acquire its modern sense of frankness.

Today's word

candid

/ˈkændɪd/ · adjective

Honest and direct, even when the truth may be uncomfortable.

Use it when

You mean open, direct communication.

A candid response says what matters without pretending the difficult part is not there.

Do not use it for

A rude comment that ignores the other person.

Candid does not excuse cruelty. The word suggests honesty with a purpose.

1

“She gave a candid account of the project’s setbacks.”

2

“We had a candid conversation about the budget.”

3

“His candid answer made the decision easier.”

A word with a visible past

From Latin candidus, “white” or “bright.” White clothing came to suggest public openness, and later candid came to describe speech that feels unguarded and sincere.

Today's word

candid

/ˈkændɪd/ · adjective

Also

frank, open

Register

Neutral

Core idea

Honesty with care

Meaning

Honest and direct, even when the truth may be uncomfortable.

Use candid when the openness is constructive: clear-eyed feedback, an unvarnished account, or a difficult but worthwhile conversation.

Examples

  • “The report is candid about the risks.”
  • “She was candid about needing more time.”
  • “A candid review can improve the next draft.”

Etymology

Latin candidus meant “white, bright, shining.” The association with public openness eventually gave English its modern word for frank speech.
